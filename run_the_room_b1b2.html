👑Leadership & Management

What makes a manager someone you actually want to work for. The most useful words and expressions are in gold.

What Makes a Good Manager

★ David Okonkwo · Leadership coach · Twenty years building teams

When I became a manager for the first time, I thought my job was to know all the answers. I was tired and stressed all the time, and my team wasn't happy. After many years of coaching other leaders, I now know I was wrong. Real authority doesn't come from having the answers. It comes from how you make your colleagues feel.

The first thing I had to learn was to delegate. To delegate means to give a job to someone else and trust them to do it. Bad managers do everything themselves. Good managers give people real responsibility. When people feel trusted, they stop doing the smallest amount of work, and they start to act like owners of the project. They become engaged.

The second thing is to build trust. The best teams are the ones where people feel safe. They can say “I don't know” or “I made a mistake” without fear. This is sometimes called psychological safety, and research shows it is the most important thing for a successful team. If people are afraid, good ideas die in silence.

“A good manager is not the loudest person in the room. A good manager is the one whose team is happy to come to work on Monday.”

Great leaders also know how to give feedback. Weak managers say nothing, or hide the truth in compliments. Strong managers are kind and clear at the same time. They say, “This isn't good enough yet, and I know you can do better.” That kind of honesty is hard to hear for a second, but it builds respect. People want to be told the truth, gently.

When something goes wrong, a good boss takes the blame in public and fixes the problem in private. They never throw a teammate under the bus. They always have your back. That's how trust is really built — not in good times, but when things are difficult.

Finally, the best managers don't micromanage. To micromanage is to check every small detail of someone else's work. It feels responsible, but it tells your team, “I don't trust you.” Be more hands-off. Give your team room. Lead by example, give honest feedback, and give credit where it's due when something goes well.

So if you're a new manager, try this in your next meeting: ask one more question than usual, listen more than you speak, and finish on time. Real leadership is quiet, kind, and patient. It's not about being the boss. It's about being the person your team wants to follow.

💸Pricing & Value

How to choose a fair price — and why the cheapest price is rarely the smartest. Key vocabulary is in gold.

Why People Don't Just Buy on Price

★ Sofia Marchetti · Pricing manager · Twenty years working with shops, cafes and apps

I help businesses choose the right price. After many years, I can tell you a surprise: people don't really buy on price alone. They buy on value. Two coffees can cost the same, but one shop is full and the other is empty. The difference is rarely the coffee itself. It is the experience, the service, the chair, the music, and how the customer feels at the end.

When times are hard, many businesses do the same thing: they cut prices. It feels safe. But it is usually a bad idea. If you keep cutting prices, your profit drops, and your customers start to expect cheap forever. Strong brands do the opposite. They charge a higher price and tell a clear story about quality, trust or design.

“Your price is the loudest message your business sends. It speaks before you do, and customers listen.”

Here is a surprising fact. Sometimes a higher price makes people more interested. A wine at €8 looks ordinary. The same wine at €25 suddenly tastes better. The price is not only what you pay; it is also information about what you get. Price is part of the story.

Inflation has changed everything. When costs go up, businesses must do something. They can raise prices, which sometimes shocks the customer. Or they can keep the price but make the product smaller — the chocolate bar that lost two squares while the price stayed the same. We call this shrinkflation. It works for a while, but customers are not stupid. Eventually they notice, and they leave.

Smart pricing is not the cheapest or the most expensive. It is about clear value for money. Some customers want the cheap option. Others happily pay more for an easier life. The big mistake is trying to win everyone. Pick your customers, then pick your price.

Before you cut prices, ask a better question: do my customers really understand what they're getting? Most of the time, the real problem is not the price. It is the story around the price. The shop is not full because the coffee is cheaper, but because somebody told the customer a story they wanted to believe. Fix the story, and you usually don't need to break the bank on big discounts at all.

📈Project Management

Why projects really fail — and how to spot trouble early. Key vocabulary is in gold.

Why Projects Go Wrong

★ Tom Aldridge · Project manager · 200 projects delivered in 15 years

Most people think a project fails in a dramatic way: a big crash, a huge problem, an angry customer. After fifteen years of running projects, I can tell you the truth is much quieter. Most projects don't fail at the end. They die in the first week, when nobody is sure what “finished” really means — and nobody asks.

The number one killer of projects has a name: scope creep. It starts small. “Could we just add one little extra feature?” Then another. Each request sounds fair, but together they slowly grow the work, and the deadline doesn't move. Soon the team is tired, the budget is gone, and nothing is finished.

The cure isn't to say no to everything. It's to start every project with everyone on the same page. The best kick-off meetings are almost boring: who decides, what “done” looks like, and what we are deliberately not doing. Five quiet minutes at the start can save five painful weeks later.

“A plan everyone ignores is worse than no plan. The point isn't the document. It's the conversation that creates it.”

I also worry when a team member says, “Don't worry, I'll do it in half the time.” It sounds great. It often isn't. To finish too fast, people cut corners, hide problems, and eventually drop the ball at the worst moment. I prefer the boring option: a small, honest plan, with the right number of people and a fair deadline.

This is why I love quick wins. A quick win is a small task you can finish fast. It shows the team that progress is possible. It also helps you find the real problems early, when there is still time to fix them. Perfect plans look beautiful and teach you nothing. Real progress is a bit messy.

Communication is everything. The best projects have a clear schedule, regular short meetings, and one person who collects what everyone has done. We call them the project manager. Their job is to ask boring questions: are we on track? Are we falling behind? What do we need this week? Without them, every project quietly drifts.

When a project goes badly — and they all do, sometimes — the worst thing a manager can do is pretend. Sometimes you have to go back to the drawing board: change the plan, talk to the customer, ask for more time, and protect the team. So measure a project not by how perfect the plan looks. Measure it by how fast bad news travels. On healthy teams, bad news arrives early, while you can still do something about it.

🎤Pitches & Presentations

How to give a presentation that people actually listen to. Key vocabulary is in gold.

Your Slides Are Not Your Presentation

★ Priya Nair · Presentation coach · Helps start-ups and managers

I've watched many smart people lose a room they should have owned. Their product was great. Their numbers were strong. And yet, somewhere around slide nineteen, you could feel the audience drift away. The painful truth is simple: your slides are not your presentation. You are.

The best presenters I coach understand that a pitch is a story, not a long list. Before they show one chart, they paint a clear picture: a real customer, a real problem, a real moment. They hook the audience in the first thirty seconds, because that is when people decide silently whether to listen or to check their phones. Start with the most interesting thing you have. Don't save it for the end. Half the room won't make it that far.

“Nobody ever said a presentation was too short. Respect the time of the room.”

Good speakers also get to the point fast. They make a clear claim, give one example, and move on. They don't read every word on every slide. Slides are not the script; they are the wallpaper behind the speaker. Each slide should help the audience, not replace the speaker.

Body language matters more than people think. Stand up straight. Slow down. Look at real faces, not at the screen behind you. A pause is your friend. Many nervous speakers fill every second with words, which makes them sound even more nervous. A calm short silence is one of the most powerful things you can do.

Then there's the skill that separates the good from the unforgettable: reading the room. You can rehearse every word, but the moment you start, you need to think on your feet. If people look confused, slow down. If they're nodding, skip ahead. A pitch isn't a recording. It's a conversation, even when only one person is talking.

When you take questions at the end, don't get defensive. Pause. A calm, short, honest answer is far better than a long, nervous one. “That's a good question. I don't know — let me check and come back to you” is a powerful sentence. Trying to fake an answer is the fastest way to lose trust.

Finally, never end with, “...so, yeah, that's it.” Every strong pitch closes with a clear call to action: exactly what you want the audience to do next. Try this in your next presentation. Cut your slides in half. Spend more time on the first 60 seconds and the last 60 seconds. Those two minutes carry more weight than everything in between. People rarely remember your data. They always remember how you made them feel.
